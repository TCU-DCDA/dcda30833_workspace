## Course Description

### Catalog Description

DCDA 30833 introduces creative coding as a method for artistic, literary, and
critical expression within digital culture. Moving beyond functional software
development, students explore code as an expressive medium capable of
dramatizing complex states of thought and feeling. Through a series of *labs*
(technical exercises) and *projects* (creative syntheses), students build
generative visual art, interactive poetry, sonified systems, and data-driven
narratives.

### Prerequisites & Concurrent Enrollment

Basic programming literacy in JavaScript or Python, or permission of the
instructor. No prior creative coding experience is required.

### Program & Major Connections

This course serves as an upper-division elective for the DCDA major and minor,
bridging technical proficiency and humanistic inquiry. It prepares students for
advanced work in digital media, interaction design, and digital humanities
research.

## Teaching Philosophy & Methodology

Code in this course is not a functional tool but a cultural material. We shift
away from *self-expression* — "what do I want to say?" — toward **system
discovery**: "what can this system reveal?" Logical structures such as loops,
conditionals, and probabilities are treated as aesthetic enactments. A
generative system, like a poem, does not *contain* a message; it **dramatizes a
state** through what it does.

Learning happens through **iterative play**. Failure is a generative data point,
not an error — Week 14 is built around exactly that premise. Constraints are
treated as engines rather than limits: a restricted palette, a single hue, a
rule without randomness, each producing more invention than an open field would.

Each unit follows the same rhythm: **Lab → Autopsy → Project.** Labs isolate one
technique. The autopsy reverse-engineers a precedent work — Molnár, Nees,
Albers, Gomringer — to see how its system operates. The project then synthesizes
both into something of your own.

Further reading:
[Theory Foundations](theory_foundations.html) ·
[Collaboration in Computer Art](collaboration.html) ·
[The Sublime and Discovery](the_sublime_and_discovery.html)

## Learning Outcomes

### Course Learning Outcomes

Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Synthesize form and content** — demonstrate how computational structures function as expressive semantic devices.
2. **Design generative systems** — create software exhibiting emergent behavior and dynamic aesthetic experience.
3. **Critique digital culture** — analyze the cultural implications of algorithms and data representation.
4. **Apply computational thinking** — solve aesthetic problems using variables, loops, functions, and objects.
5. **Iterate and refine** — employ an iterative design process that prioritizes discovery over predetermined outcomes.

### Program & Major Learning Outcomes

This course serves the following DCDA program outcomes:

1. Students will critically assess the social and/or historical contexts of
   digital cultures by interrogating the norms and conventions of those cultures
   and the values they represent.
2. Students will demonstrate an ability to compose digital arguments.

The first is carried by the precedent autopsies, which situate a system in the
moment that produced it, and by the data unit (Weeks 12–13), where mapping and
normalization are treated as value-laden acts rather than neutral ones. The
second is carried by every project: each is a working system published to the
open web and accompanied by an artist statement that argues for what it does.

### TCU Outcomes: Core Curriculum & Finding Ourselves in Community

Not applicable. DCDA 30833 is an upper-division elective and carries neither
Core Curriculum nor Finding Ourselves in Community (FSC) designation.

Work in this course comes in two forms, on a consistent rhythm: **a week's
technique is taught Monday, its lab is due that Wednesday, and a second lab —
where there is one — the following Monday. Projects are due Wednesday.** You

Every lab and project is **published live**. GitHub Pages is enabled in Week 1
and serves your work for the rest of the semester, so each assignment is pushed
*and* reachable at its own URL. Deployment is routine here, not a finals-week
scramble.

### Quizzes & Exams

There are none. All assessment is project-based.

### Formal Writing & Research

Each major project is accompanied by a short **artist statement** (300–500
words) articulating the conceptual stakes of the system and the decisions behind
its behavior — what the system does, not what you meant by it.

### Labs, Presentations & Critiques

**Critique is central.** We run code live in class and discuss it as an
aesthetic object, asking what the system dramatizes rather than whether it
succeeded. Giving and receiving feedback is required work, not a formality.

### Final Evaluative Exercise

**Final Project: Expressive Generative System** — a major, self-directed work
synthesizing the semester's concepts, presented publicly during our assigned
final exam block (May 10–14). It is followed by the **Final Portfolio**: your
complete body of work on GitHub Pages, with a reflection.

## Grading Philosophy & Policy

### Course Assignments & Final Grade

Because this course is ungraded, no assignment carries a point value or a
percentage weight. The full set of work expected of you is:

| Deliverable | Count | Cadence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Labs | 14 (LAB 0–13) | Most weeks; see schedule |
| Projects | 6 | Roughly every other week, due Wednesday |
| Artist statements | One per project | With each project |
| Critique participation | Ongoing | Throughout |
| Final Project | 1 | Finals period |
| Final Portfolio & self-evaluation | 1 | Finals week |

Your final grade is arrived at through the self-evaluation described below,
based on the whole of that record.

### Grading Scale(s)

This course uses an **ungrading** model. Individual assignments do not receive
letter grades. Instead you receive detailed qualitative feedback on code and
concept, and you reflect regularly on your own progress.

At the end of the semester you submit your portfolio together with a
**self-evaluation proposing your final grade**, grounded in your growth, your
effort, and the quality of the work. If my assessment differs significantly from
yours, we meet and discuss it.

**Why ungrading?** Creative coding requires risk-taking and productive failure.
Traditional grading punishes failure, which drives students toward safe,
uninteresting work. Ungrading is meant to make experiment affordable — to let
you break things and learn from the wreckage without paying for it in points.

### Late Work

- **Labs:** accepted up to one week late. The point is completion and learning, not penalty.
- **Projects:** should arrive on time for critique. A late project may miss its critique window, which costs you feedback and costs your classmates your work — the real penalty, and not one I impose.

### Participation, Engagement & Attendance

Coding is a practice, not a theory. Much of our time is studio time, where
debugging happens with other people in the room. Attendance matters here for the
same reason it matters in a studio art course: the work happens in the room.

## Course Policies

### Artificial Intelligence

AI is treated in this course as **creative material and collaborator**, not as a
shortcut. You may use it — LAB 7 requires it — and you must document and cite
it. The governing standard is the **Explanation Test:** you must be able to
explain every line of code you submit.

Full policy: [AI in This Course — Policy & Practice](ai_policy.html). Read it
alongside [Collaboration in Computer Art](collaboration.html), which situates AI
as the newest register of a collaboration you were always already in — with the
medium, the community, language, and art history.

### Working from Examples

Looking at other people's systems is how this discipline is learned; the
precedent autopsies build it into the course.
[Working from Examples](working_from_examples.html) sets out how to work from an
example without submitting it.

### Technology

Bring your laptop to every class. All required software is free.
