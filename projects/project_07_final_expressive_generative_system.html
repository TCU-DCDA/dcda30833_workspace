FINAL PROJECT — Expressive Generative System

Course: DCDA 30833 — Creative Coding for the Humanities Due: Finals period, May 10–14 — public exhibition at our assigned block Studio: Week 15 (final build Monday; in-progress critique Wednesday) Tools: Your choice, from anything the course has used Submission: GitHub link to your project folder + artist statement; live on GitHub Pages

Purpose

This is the self-directed work the semester has been building toward: one system, of your own design, that behaves like something.

Everything before this told you what to constrain. This one asks you to choose the constraint — which is the harder half, and the reason it comes last.

The exhibition is not a formality. Your system runs in front of people who did not build it, on a machine that is not yours, from a URL. That has been true of every assignment since Week 1; this is the one where it matters.

Precedents — System Autopsy (In-Class)

How examples work in this course: you will always be shown examples — but here, an example is a system to take apart, not an image to reproduce. You'll see how a work was made, and the range of outcomes its rules can produce, more often than a single finished "right answer." There is no result to match: a strong system can generate many different outcomes, including ones no one has made yet.

Why take other makers' work apart? Because you are always collaborating with what came before — the autopsy just makes it deliberate. See Collaboration in Computer Art.

The precedents for this project are different in kind. Rather than a technique, each is an artist who stayed with one system long enough to find out what it contained — which is the question a final project puts to you in miniature.

Look at:

  • Vera Molnár: decades spent on a small set of constraints — squares, lines, controlled disorder — treating each work as another run of a continuing investigation rather than a separate statement. Her phrase for the disruption she introduced was 1% disorder.
  • Harold Cohen — AARON: a drawing program he developed and lived with from 1973 until his death in 2016. Over forty years the system learned to draw differently, and Cohen kept describing himself as its collaborator rather than its author.
  • Casey Reas — Software Structures: one written structure, implemented several ways, exhibited side by side — the clearest available demonstration that the system and its output are different objects.

Where to find them (free):

  • Vera Molnár — our live Interruptions recreation and a p5.js walkthrough of Structures de Quadrilatères
  • Harold Cohen — the Computer History Museum's AARON materials
  • Casey Reas — Software Structures at the Whitney's artport

Autopsy questions (ask of each):

  1. What is the rule, stated as an instruction someone else could follow?
  2. What is fixed, and where does chance or judgment enter?
  3. Which single constraint is doing the most work?
  4. What state or behavior does the system dramatize, setting aside "what it means"?
  5. What would have to change for it to stop being the same system?

In class: examine 2–3 of these together at the project's launch, then carry the same questions into your own build.

Core Requirements

Your system must:

  • Be generative — it produces variation from rules, and you do not place its parts by hand
  • Have a stated constraint you imposed on yourself, named plainly in the README
  • Hold a recognizable identity across runs: two viewings should be different and evidently the same system
  • Dramatize a state through behavior rather than illustrate a subject
  • Run in a browser from your GitHub Pages URL, on someone else's machine, with no setup and no explanation from you

It must also draw on at least two of the course's four materials — image, text, sound, data. Not as a checklist: because the interesting systems this semester were the ones where a rule from one material got applied to another.

Scope

This is a two-week build with a critique in the middle, not a capstone thesis.

A good final project here is one clear system explored properly, not four half-systems in a tab bar. If you are choosing between adding a feature and understanding what your system already does, choose the second — it is what the artist statement will need.

Reusing your own earlier work is encouraged: extend a LAB 13 keep, deepen a PROJECT 5 phase system, translate something further than LAB 12 took it. Starting fresh is also fine. What is not fine is starting fresh and starting late.

Artist Statement (400–600 words)

Not a description of what you made. An account of what you found.

Address:

  • The system: state the rule so that someone could reimplement it.
  • The constraint: what you refused yourself, and what that refusal produced.
  • The discovery: something the system does that you did not plan. If there is nothing, say so honestly — that is a finding about the system, and about how tightly you specified it.
  • The state it dramatizes: what condition its behavior performs. Resist explaining what it means; describe what it does, and let the reader draw the rest.
  • The lineage: which precedents, techniques, or classmates' work this is in conversation with. Per the course's framing, you are always collaborating — name it.

Working with AI

The terms from PROJECT 5 and PROJECT 6 apply — see AI in This Course.

At this point you have used AI as material (LAB 7), as a collaborator on a sound system (PROJECT 5), and as a data assistant with its own quiet agenda (PROJECT 6). For the final, the requirement is simply that the account be complete:

  • ai_log.md — tool, prompts, what you kept, what you rejected and why
  • The Explanation Test — you can explain every line you submit, and you will be asked to, at the exhibition
  • Using no AI is a documented methodological choice; state it in the README

Technical Requirements

  • Any of: p5.js, Tone.js, CSV/JSON data, the DOM
  • Everything committed to your repo — no live APIs that may be down at the exhibition, no local file paths, no assets outside the repo
  • Runs on first load; if it needs a click to start audio, say so on the canvas
  • Tested from the public URL, on a machine that is not yours, before the exhibition. Case-sensitivity in file paths is the classic failure here.

Process Documentation Requirements

Your project folder must include:

  • index.html, your sketch files, and any data
  • README.md: the rule, the constraint, what to look for, how long to watch, and any sources or data citations
  • statement.md: your artist statement
  • ai_log.md, if applicable

Reflection

The reflection for this project is the artist statement. The broader looking-back happens in the Final Portfolio.

How This Project Fits the Course Arc

It is the argument of the course, made by you rather than to you: that form is not a container for content but performs it, that systems dramatize states rather than carry messages, and that constraint generates possibility instead of removing it.

What follows is the Final Portfolio, where you assemble the semester and assess it yourself.
