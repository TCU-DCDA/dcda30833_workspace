FINAL PORTFOLIO — Published Work & Self-Evaluation

Course: DCDA 30833 — Creative Coding for the Humanities Due: Finals week Tools: GitHub Pages (already running since Week 1) Submission: The URL of your published portfolio

Purpose

This is where the semester becomes a body of work rather than a folder of assignments.

You have been publishing since Week 1 — every lab and project has a live URL. The portfolio is not a new deployment; it is the act of selecting, arranging, and accounting for what is already there.

It is also where your grade comes from. This course is ungraded throughout, and the self-evaluation here is the mechanism that resolves that. See the syllabus for the full description.

Part A: The Published Portfolio

A single page on your GitHub Pages site that collects your semester.

It must include:

  • At least six works, live and running, each linked and each labeled with what it is. Include all six major projects if they are in shape; substitute a lab you are proud of for a project you are not.
  • A one-to-two sentence note per work — what the system does, not what you intended. The note should help a stranger know what to look at and how long to watch.
  • Your Final Project, given the most room.
  • Working links. Every URL resolves, on a machine that is not yours.

Arrangement is part of the assignment. Chronological is the obvious choice and rarely the best one. Grouping by material, by constraint, or by what the systems have in common will tell a truer story about your semester than the calendar does.

You may build the page however you like. It can itself be generative — several of you will find that the most honest option.

Part B: The Reflection (600–900 words)

Written for someone who was not in the room. Address:

  1. The arc. What changed between LAB 0 and the Final Project? Be specific about a capability or a way of thinking, not "I learned a lot."

  2. The turn. The course's central claim is discovery over expression — moving from "what do I want to say?" to "what can this system reveal?" Did that land for you? Name the assignment where it did, or make the case that it didn't and say what you think is wrong with it. Both are acceptable answers; only the unexamined one isn't.

  3. The constraint that worked. Somewhere this semester a restriction produced more than freedom would have. Which, and what did it produce?

  4. The failure that mattered. Something broke and you kept it, or something broke and you learned from losing it.

  5. Collaboration. Per Collaboration in Computer Art, you were always collaborating — with the medium, with precedents, with classmates in critique, with AI where you used it. Give an honest account of the collaborations that shaped this work, including which ones you leaned on too hard.

  6. What you would build next, if the semester continued.

Part C: The Self-Evaluation

This course does not grade individual assignments. At the end, you propose your final grade.

Write 300–500 words making the case, grounded in evidence from your portfolio rather than in effort alone. Useful things to weigh:

  • Growth — the distance between your early and late work
  • Engagement — with the labs, with critique, with other people's systems
  • Risk — where you tried something that might not have worked, and what happened
  • Follow-through — whether the work is finished, published, and documented
  • Quality — of the systems themselves, judged as systems

Be accurate rather than modest or generous. Students routinely under-propose; that is not humility, it is a less useful self-assessment. If you think the evidence supports an A, say so and show the evidence.

If my assessment differs significantly from yours, we will meet and talk it through. That conversation is part of the design, not a penalty.

What "Done" Looks Like

A Note on Publishing

You enabled GitHub Pages in Week 1 and have deployed all semester. That was deliberate: publication has been a condition of the work rather than an event at the end of it.

The consequence is that this portfolio is a public artifact you keep. It will outlast the course, and it is a reasonable thing to show someone — an employer, a graduate program, a collaborator. Build it as something you would be willing to send.
