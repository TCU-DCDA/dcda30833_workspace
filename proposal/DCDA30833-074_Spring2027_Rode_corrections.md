## Course Description

DCDA 30833 introduces creative coding as a method for artistic, literary, and
critical expression within digital culture. Moving beyond functional software
development, students explore code as an expressive medium capable of
dramatizing complex states of thought and feeling. Through a series of "labs"
(technical exercises) and "projects" (creative syntheses), students will build
generative visual art, interactive poetry, sonified systems, and data-driven
narratives.

## Teaching Philosophy & Methodology

**Form = Content · Systems Dramatize States · Discovery over Expression**

My teaching philosophy posits that code is not merely a functional tool but a
cultural material. In this course, we shift away from "self-expression" (what do
I want to say?) towards system discovery (what can this system reveal?). We treat
logical structures—loops, conditionals, probabilities—as aesthetic enactments.
Learning occurs through iterative play, treating failure as a generative data
point rather than an error.

Each unit follows the same rhythm: **Lab → Autopsy → Project.** Labs isolate one
technique. The System Autopsy reverse-engineers a precedent work — Molnár, Nees,
Albers, Gomringer — to see how its system operates. The project then synthesizes
both into something of your own.

## Grading Philosophy & Policy

This course utilizes an **Ungrading** philosophy. This means I will not assign
letter grades to individual assignments. Instead, we will focus on qualitative
feedback, self-reflection, and iterative improvement.

- **Feedback:** You will receive detailed feedback on your code and concepts.
- **Self-Evaluation:** You will regularly reflect on your own learning and
  progress through the course of the semester, culminating in the Final
  Portfolio self-evaluation.
- **Final Grade:** At the end of the semester, you will submit a portfolio and a
  self-evaluation proposing your final grade based on your growth, effort, and
  the quality of your work. If my assessment differs significantly from yours,
  we will meet to discuss it. The final grade is recorded on the University
  letter-grade scale below.

**Why Ungrading?** Creative coding requires risk-taking and failure. A
traditional grading system often punishes failure, leading to safe, boring work.
Ungrading encourages you to experiment, break things, and learn from the process
without fear of a low grade.

### Artificial Intelligence (AI / LLM) Use Policy

**Stance: embraced, with documentation.** In this course AI is treated as
creative material and collaborator, not as a shortcut. You may use AI tools
(ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, etc.), and one lab requires it. The governing standard
is the **Explanation Test**: you must be able to explain every line of code you
submit.

- **Labs — by hand.** Labs build your grammar; using AI to complete one skips the
  learning the lab exists to create. The exception is the lab that is *about* AI:
  LAB 7 — Prompt as Material.
- **Projects — welcome where invited.** From PROJECT 4 onward, AI is welcome as
  material: a system to negotiate with and curate, not a vending machine.
- **The Explanation Test.** Before you keep anything AI gives you, you should be
  able to (1) explain what it does and why it's there, (2) run it and see what it
  actually does, and (3) recreate or modify it yourself. Never submit code or
  text you cannot explain.
- **Show your collaboration.** When a lab or project uses AI, keep a short AI log
  in your repository — a few annotated moments: your prompt, what the AI gave
  you, and why it mattered. Credit AI in your `README.md` (which tool, used for
  what), and mark AI-assisted code with a comment, e.g.
  `// AI-assisted: asked for a particle loop; I changed the decay rule.`
- **Writing is yours.** Artist statements and your portfolio reflection must be
  in your own words. You may ask AI for feedback on a draft; note it at the end of
  the piece.
- **Opting out** of AI is a legitimate, documented choice, not an omission.
